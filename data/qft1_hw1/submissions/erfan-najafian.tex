\section*{1. Lattice and Paths}

For a $d$-dimensional lattice, we define

\begin{equation}
    x^\mu = n^\mu \Delta,
    \qquad
    n^\mu \in \mathbb{Z}^d .
\end{equation}

For example, in two dimensions there are two independent lattice
directions,

\begin{equation}
    \hat e_1,\qquad \hat e_2,
\end{equation}

and in three dimensions,

\begin{equation}
    \hat e_1,\qquad
    \hat e_2,\qquad
    \hat e_3.
\end{equation}

In general, for four dimensions,

\begin{equation}
    \hat e_i,
    \qquad i=1,2,3,4.
\end{equation}

The number of independent directions in $d$ dimensions is $d$.
For example,

\begin{equation}
    d=2
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \mu=1,2,
\end{equation}

while

\begin{equation}
    d=3
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \mu=1,2,3.
\end{equation}

Thus, in general,

\begin{equation}
    x^\mu=n^\mu\Delta,
    \qquad
    n^\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^d.
\end{equation}

The Green function can be represented as a sum over all paths
connecting the origin to $x$:

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    G(m_0,x)
    =
    \sum_{0\to x}
    \ee^{-m_0\,\ell(\text{path})}
    }
\end{equation}

where $\ell(\text{path})$ denotes the length of the path.

\section*{2. Example}

Consider a path from $A$ to $C$. There are two shortest paths:

\begin{equation}
    A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow C,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    A\longrightarrow D\longrightarrow C.
\end{equation}

The corresponding diagram is

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering

\coordinate (B) at (0,2);
\coordinate (C) at (4,2);

\node[left] at (B) {$B$};
\node[right] at (C) {$C$};
\node[right] at (D) {$D$};

\end{tikzpicture}

\caption{Two shortest paths from $A$ to $C$.}
\end{figure}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
    A\to C
    \equiv
    \left\{
    \begin{array}{l}
    A\to B\to C,\\[2mm]
    A\to D\to C.
    \end{array}
    \right.
\end{equation}

Each shortest path has two lattice steps, so that

\begin{equation}
    \ell(\text{path})=2\Delta .
\end{equation}

Hence the contribution from these two paths is

\begin{align}
    G
    &=
    \sum_{A\to C}
    \ee^{-m_0\ell(\text{path})}
    \\
    &=
    \ee^{-m_0(2\Delta)}
    +
    \ee^{-m_0(2\Delta)}
    \\
    &=
    2\ee^{-2m_0\Delta}.
\end{align}

More generally, let $N_j(n)$ denote the number of paths of length
$j$ connecting the origin to the lattice point $n$. Then

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    G(m_0,n)
    =
    \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
    N_j(n)\,
    \ee^{-m_0j\Delta}
    }
\end{equation}

% =================================================
% 3. Continuum limit
% =================================================

\section*{3. Continuum Limit}

Since

\begin{equation}
    n=\frac{x}{\Delta},
\end{equation}

we can write

\begin{equation}
    G(x,m_0)
    =
    \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
    N_j\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)
    \ee^{-m_0j\Delta}.
\end{equation}

In the continuum limit,

\begin{equation}
    \Delta\rightarrow0,
\end{equation}

we therefore have

\begin{equation}
    G(x,m_0)
    =
    \lim_{\Delta\to0}
    \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
    N_j\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)
    \ee^{-m_0j\Delta}.
\end{equation}

The number of paths grows as the lattice becomes finer. Therefore,
the mass parameter has to be treated carefully in the continuum
limit. In particular, we allow

\begin{equation}
    m_0=m_0(\Delta).
\end{equation}

The small-$\Delta$ expansion is

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    m_0(\Delta)
    =
    m_0(0)
    +
    \left.
    \frac{dm_0}{d\Delta}
    \right|_{\Delta=0}\Delta
    +\mathcal{O}(\Delta^2)
    }.
\end{equation}

% =================================================
% 4. Expansion of the exponential
% =================================================

\section*{4. Expansion}

We have

\begin{align}
    \ee^{-m_0(\Delta)j\Delta}
    &=
    \ee^{-m_0(0)j\Delta}
    \exp\left[
        -j\Delta
        \left(
        \left.
        \frac{dm_0}{d\Delta}
        \right|_{\Delta=0}\Delta
        +\cdots
        \right)
    \right].
\end{align}

Keeping the leading terms,

\begin{equation}
    \ee^{-m_0(\Delta)j\Delta}
    \simeq
    \ee^{-m_0(0)j\Delta}
    \left[
        1
        -
        j
        \left.
        \frac{dm_0}{d\Delta}
        \right|_{\Delta=0}
        \Delta
        +\cdots
    \right].
\end{equation}

Consequently,

\begin{align}
G(x,m_0)
\simeq
&
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
\ee^{-m_0(0)j\Delta}
\left[
N_j\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)
+
jN_j\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)
\left.
\frac{dm_0}{d\Delta}
\right|_{\Delta=0}
\Delta
\right]
\\
&\qquad
+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^2).
\end{align}

The change in the number of paths can be written schematically as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta N_j
    \simeq
    x\frac{dN_j}{dn}.
\end{equation}

Thus the behavior of $N_j(n)$ as $n\to\infty$ becomes important in
the continuum limit.

% =================================================
% 5. Fourier representation
% =================================================

\section*{5. Fourier Representation}

For a translationally invariant lattice, one may introduce a Fourier
representation of the path-counting function. Schematically,

\begin{equation}
    \ee^{ik_\mu x^\mu}
    =
    f(x),
\end{equation}

and the behavior of $N_j(n)$ for large $n$ determines the continuum
behavior of the Green function.

The important point is that as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta\rightarrow0,
\end{equation}

the lattice point

\begin{equation}
    n=\frac{x}{\Delta}
\end{equation}

goes to infinity for fixed nonzero $x$. Therefore, the asymptotic
growth of $N_j(n)$ has to be compensated by the exponential factor
in the propagator.

% =================================================
% 6. Number of paths
% =================================================

\section*{6. Growth of the Number of Paths}

Consider the Green function

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    G(m_0,x)
    =
    \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
    N_j\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)
    \ee^{-m_0j\Delta}
    }.
\end{equation}

A path of length $j$ contains $j$ elementary steps. In two
dimensions there are four possible nearest-neighbor directions,

\begin{equation}
    \pm\hat e_1,
    \qquad
    \pm\hat e_2,
\end{equation}

so that at each step there are at most

\begin{equation}
    2d=4
\end{equation}

possible choices.

In the same way, in four dimensions the number of nearest-neighbor
directions is

\begin{equation}
    2d=8.
\end{equation}

Thus the number of possible paths grows exponentially. For the
four-dimensional case,

\begin{equation}
    N_{2L}(x)\gtrsim 8^L,
\end{equation}

and in particular,

\begin{equation}
    N_{2L}(0)\gtrsim 8^L.
\end{equation}

This exponential growth is the source of the divergence of the
path sum unless the exponential suppression from the factor
$\ee^{-m_0j\Delta}$ is sufficiently strong.

% =================================================
% 7. Divergence condition
% =================================================

\section*{7. Condition for Convergence}

Starting from

\begin{equation}
    G(m_0,x)
    =
    \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}
    N_j(n)\,
    \ee^{-m_0j\Delta},
\end{equation}

and using the estimate

\begin{equation}
    N_{2L}(x)\gtrsim 8^L,
\end{equation}

we obtain

\begin{align}
    G(m_0,x)
    &\gtrsim
    \sum_{L=0}^{\infty}
    8^L
    \ee^{-m_0\Delta(2L)}
    \\
    &=
    \sum_{L=0}^{\infty}
    \left(
        8\ee^{-2m_0\Delta}
    \right)^L.
\end{align}

Therefore, convergence requires

\begin{equation}
    8\ee^{-2m_0\Delta}<1.
\end{equation}

Equivalently,

\begin{align}
    \ee^{-2m_0\Delta}
    &<
    \frac{1}{8},
\end{align}

which gives

\begin{align}
    -2m_0\Delta
    &<
    -\ln 8,
\end{align}

and hence

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    m_0
    >
    \frac{\ln 8}{2\Delta}
    }.
\end{equation}

Thus the bare mass parameter must grow appropriately as
$\Delta\rightarrow0$ in order to suppress the exponential growth
of the number of lattice paths.

Hence the Green function is the weighted sum

\begin{equation}
    G(m_0,x)
    =
    \sum_{\text{paths }0\to x}
    \ee^{-m_0\ell(\text{path})}.
\end{equation}

Each path is weighted by its length, and the factor
$\ee^{-m_0\ell}$ suppresses long paths.
